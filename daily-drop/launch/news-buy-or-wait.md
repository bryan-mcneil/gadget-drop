There is a specific, expensive mistake almost every gadget buyer makes at least once: paying full price for something three weeks before its replacement is announced. The product does not get worse. The price does. Within a month of a launch, the outgoing model is usually discounted, and the person who bought early paid a premium for the privilege of waiting.

Starting today, GadgetDrop tracks that timing the same way we already track prices. Our new [buy-or-wait pages](/buy-or-wait) record when each product line we cover last refreshed, how often it has historically refreshed, and a link to the manufacturer's own announcement for every date on the page.

## What Release Timing Actually Costs You

The pattern is not subtle once you look at it line by line, and it varies far more than most buyers assume.

Apple has announced a new iPhone in September every year since 2012, with a single exception: the iPhone 12, which slipped to October 13, 2020. That is close enough to a twelve month clock to plan around, and it means a July purchase sits roughly ten months into a twelve month cycle. AirPods Pro, sold in the same store by the same company, run on a completely different schedule: October 2019, September 2022, September 2025. That is a three year cadence, so ten months after a launch is early, not late. Identical timing, opposite advice, and the only way to know which is which is to have the release history written down.

Then there are the lines that break their own pattern. GoPro shipped a HERO flagship almost every September for nearly a decade, and then did not ship one in 2025 at all. Anyone who "waited for the new one" that autumn waited through a year that never came. That is exactly why our pages report what has shipped rather than what is expected to ship.

Samsung's Galaxy S line offers a cleaner example of a moving target. Unpacked has landed in January or February every year since 2019, but the S26 was unveiled on February 25, 2026 and reached shelves two weeks after that. A cadence is a pattern, not a promise, and the difference matters when you are deciding whether to buy this weekend.

## How the Verdicts Work

Each page crosses two things and nothing else: where the line sits in its cycle, and where today's price sits against our own recorded price history for the product we review on that line. No manufacturer list prices, no leaks, no supply chain reports.

That produces four possible answers. "Buy now" means early in the cycle and priced below its own tracked typical price. "Wait for the refresh" means late in the cycle, where a successor tends to drag the outgoing model's price down and waiting pays twice. "Wait for a better price" means there is no new model coming soon but the current price is running high. And "no strong signal" means mid cycle at an ordinary price, which is the honest answer most of the year for most products.

Every verdict also carries a confidence level and the date a human last checked those release dates against their source. If nobody has re-verified a line in six months, the page says so in a banner above the verdict and the confidence drops automatically. Stale timing advice is worse than none, so we made staleness visible rather than hiding it.

One thing we deliberately do not do is rumours. There are no predicted release dates on these pages, no "sources familiar with the matter", and no unannounced products. That makes us less specific than a rumour blog. It also means we are not confidently wrong four times a year.

## Buy or Wait?

If you are shopping right now, check the line before you check the price. A product that is ten months into a twelve month cycle is worth waiting on almost regardless of the discount, because the discount is coming anyway. A product that is ten months into a three year cycle is fine to buy today, and the only question left is whether the current price is good, which our [tracked price drops](/deals) page answers separately.

The one case where those two signals genuinely conflict is a late cycle product sitting at the lowest price we have ever recorded for it. Clearance pricing on an outgoing model can beat waiting for a successor that will launch at full price. Our verdicts call that out as a real either/or instead of pretending there is one right answer.
