Ring cameras can now put a name to the person at your door. The company has started rolling out Familiar Faces, an AI facial-recognition feature, to Ring owners across the United States, and it changes the calculation on whether a Ring is worth buying. The feature is genuinely useful. It's also locked behind Ring's most expensive subscription, and it's switched off entirely in a handful of places because of privacy law.

## What Ring's Familiar Faces Actually Does

Familiar Faces uses on-camera AI to recognize people who show up regularly and lets you build a catalog of up to 50 labeled faces: family, roommates, the mail carrier, a regular dog walker. Once you tag someone, their name replaces the generic alert. Instead of "person detected at front door," your phone says "Mom at front door," and that name follows through to the app timeline and Event History too.

The catch is the price of entry. Familiar Faces requires Ring's top-tier plan, Ring Home Premium, which runs about $20 a month. The cheaper Ring subscriptions don't include it. Ring is also holding the feature back in states and provinces with strict biometric-privacy rules: it's unavailable to customers in Illinois, Texas and Portland, Oregon, and to Canadian customers in Quebec. Everywhere else it's opt-in, so you have to switch it on yourself, and you can leave it off.

Ring paired the launch with Search Party, a related feature that scans nearby opted-in Ring cameras to help neighbors find a lost dog. Both lean on the same AI push the company has been building out over the past year.

## What It Means for Buyers

For the right household, this is a real quality-of-life upgrade. If your camera pings you twenty times a day and most of those are your own family coming and going, named alerts cut the noise down to the ones that matter. People who already pay for Ring Home Premium get it at no extra cost, so there's little reason not to try it.

For everyone else, the value is thinner than the headline suggests. The feature is gated to the priciest plan, so a lot of Ring owners on the entry subscription are locked out unless they upgrade, and paying roughly $20 a month for smarter notifications is a hard sell when a doorbell is a set-and-forget purchase. If you live in one of the excluded states, the decision is made for you. And if the idea of a company storing a catalog of faces from your front porch gives you pause, that's a fair reason to skip it, biometric data is not something you can easily claw back once it's collected.

There's a bigger pattern here worth clocking. Ring keeps parking its best AI, first Smart Video Search, now Familiar Faces, inside its top subscription tier. The hardware is the cheap part; the ongoing fee is where the value lives. If you're shopping for a doorbell, buy it for the camera and the price, not for a feature you'll rent. Our take on the [Ring Battery Doorbell (2nd gen)](/posts/ring-battery-doorbell-2nd-gen-review-2k-on-a-budget) still holds: it's a strong 2K doorbell on its own, and the AI extras are a bonus only if you were already paying for the top plan.

## Buy or Wait?

If you already subscribe to Ring Home Premium and you're outside the excluded regions, turn Familiar Faces on. It's a free upgrade that makes your existing alerts meaningfully smarter, and you can disable it any time it feels like too much.

If you're eyeing a new Ring specifically for facial recognition, wait, or at least separate the two decisions. Buy the doorbell for the camera quality and the price we track, then decide on the subscription with clear eyes. Do not jump to the $20-a-month tier for this feature alone. Competing cameras bundle person and package alerts for less, and the named-face trick, useful as it is, isn't worth doubling your monthly bill on its own.
