## Body (paste into the post body field)

"You get what you pay for" is half true. Sometimes the expensive option is genuinely better in ways you'll feel every day. Other times you're paying for a logo, a spec you'll never use, or a number that looks good on a box. The skill isn't being cheap *or* being a brand loyalist — it's knowing which categories reward spending and which don't. Here's the framework I use, and how it plays out across the gadgets people actually buy.

### The one question that decides it

Before any purchase, ask: **"Will I feel the difference every single day?"**

- If yes — the thing you touch, wear, look at, or rely on constantly — premium is often worth it. The cost spreads across thousands of small moments.
- If no — the thing you use occasionally, or where "good enough" is genuinely good enough — budget usually wins, and the savings are real.

That single question sorts most decisions. The rest is applying it honestly to each category.

### Where premium usually pays off

These are the categories where spending more tends to buy something you'll actually notice:

- **Anything you wear or hold for hours.** A chair, a keyboard, headphones, a phone. Comfort, build quality, and ergonomics compound over time. A cheap keyboard you type on all day is a false economy.
- **Screens you stare at.** A good monitor or a phone display is in front of your eyes constantly. Better panels, brightness, and colour are a daily quality-of-life upgrade.
- **Things where failure is expensive.** A reliable power bank, a trustworthy storage drive, a solid charger. Saving $15 on the component protecting your data or your devices is the wrong place to economise.
- **Cameras, if you actually shoot.** The gap between a budget and premium camera is real — *if* you use it. If it lives in a drawer, that gap is wasted money.

The theme: premium rewards **frequency** and **stakes**. High use or high cost-of-failure justifies the spend.

> The honest take: the best value isn't the cheapest price or the flashiest flagship — it's the point where extra money stops buying things you'll notice. That point is different in every category, and finding it is the whole game.

### Where budget usually wins

Just as often, the smart money is the cheaper option:

- **Cables and basic accessories.** A reputable budget USB-C cable moves the same data and power as a premium one. Buy on safety and reviews, not brand.
- **"Latest model" upgrades.** Last year's flagship is frequently 90% of this year's at 60% of the price. New tech carries an early-adopter tax.
- **Specs you can't perceive.** Refresh rates beyond what your eyes track, audio codecs your phone can't use, resolutions invisible at normal distance. If you can't tell the difference, you're paying for a number.
- **Single-use or occasional gear.** A gadget for a one-off project or a feature you'll touch twice a year doesn't deserve a premium.
- **Fast-moving or easily-lost items.** Things you'll replace or misplace anyway (cheap trackers, basic earbuds for the gym) don't reward a big investment.

### The traps that make smart people overspend

A few patterns separate good buyers from impulse buyers:

1. **Buying for the version of yourself you imagine, not the one you are.** The pro camera "for when I get serious," the standing desk you'll "definitely use." Buy for your real habits.
2. **Spec-sheet hypnosis.** Bigger numbers feel like value. Ask whether *you* will ever reach them.
3. **The flagship halo.** A brand's best product being great doesn't mean its mid-range is the best mid-range. Judge each tier on its own.
4. **Ignoring total cost.** A cheap printer with expensive ink, a budget phone with no updates — the sticker price isn't the real price.

### A quick category cheat-sheet

If you want the short version, here's roughly where the value tends to land:

- **Spend up:** headphones/earbuds you use daily, a monitor, a chair, storage and power gear, a phone if it's your main device.
- **Go mid-range:** most laptops, most smartwatches, tablets, routers — the sweet spot is usually one tier below the top.
- **Buy budget:** cables, chargers (from reputable brands), gym earbuds, occasional-use gadgets, last-gen flagships.

None of this is absolute — your habits override every rule here. Someone who lives in their headphones and barely glances at their watch should invert the "wearables" advice entirely. The framework just forces the honest question instead of letting the marketing answer it for you.
